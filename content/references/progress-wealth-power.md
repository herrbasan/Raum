# Research Notes: Progress, Wealth, and Power

## Broad long-run improvement

Steven Pinker's *The Better Angels of Our Nature* and *Enlightenment Now* assemble evidence for long-run improvements across dimensions including violence, health, longevity, poverty, literacy, and material capability.

The relevant use is narrower than “everything got better for everyone.” Long-run improvement can coexist with reversals, unequal outcomes, environmental costs, and substantial private wealth. Pinker's books are not evidence that every form of wealth creation benefits everyone.

## Inequality did not move in one direction

The World Inequality Report's long-run series show top income shares declining through much of the middle of the twentieth century and rising again after 1980. The timing and magnitude vary by country and by whether income or wealth is measured.

Broad progress therefore cannot be used to claim that concentration rose uniformly while everyone's life improved. It has coincided with both compression and renewed concentration. Public investment, organized political struggle, redistribution, and changing rules helped shape those outcomes alongside invention and markets.

## Positive-sum and extractive wealth

Wealth can be created by expanding useful capabilities: better health, cheaper clean energy, improved education, infrastructure, and services. This supports a positive-sum offer in which builders and investors prosper by improving other people's lives.

Wealth can also be captured through monopoly rents, restricted access, externalized environmental damage, land scarcity, or political privilege. The availability of a positive-sum path does not establish that it is the default or that every fortune satisfies it.

## Absolute capability and positional advantage

Health, security, time, and practical freedom can improve for one person without requiring another person to have less. Status and power over others are relational: not everyone can occupy the top position or dominate everyone else.

Additional income can continue to improve aspects of well-being, although its marginal effect varies. The important distinction is not a claimed plateau in happiness but the difference between reproducible capabilities and advantages whose value depends on comparison or control.

Luxury also is not automatically separable from ecological allocation. Some luxuries use scarce land, energy, materials, or human attention and therefore compete with other uses. The boundary between acceptable luxury and harmful domination requires ecological budgets and institutions that limit the conversion of wealth into political control.

## What this argument does not support

It does not establish that wealth is inherently illegitimate, that all progress depended on markets alone, that redistribution never mattered, or that status competition can be eliminated. It supports a narrower claim: becoming rich by expanding human capability is possible, while access to basic dignity need not depend on relative rank.

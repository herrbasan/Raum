# Research Notes: Religion as a Cognitive Byproduct

## The claim

The cognitive science of religion (CSR) holds that religious thought is not an adaptation and not a separate faculty. It is what ordinary cognition produces when its standard tools run past their proper objects — a byproduct of machinery that evolved for other jobs. The school has been mainstream since the 1990s.

## The machinery

- **Anthropomorphism (Guthrie).** Stewart Guthrie's founding move: perceiving minds and faces in clouds, rustles, and storms is a systematic perceptual strategy, not an error rate. When in doubt, bet on an agent — the false positive is cheap, the missed predator is not. *Faces in the Clouds* (1993) is the canonical statement; the argument goes back to his 1980 articles.
- **Hyperactive agency detection (Barrett).** Justin Barrett's HADD: the cognitive system that detects intentional agents is tuned so sensitively that it fires on ambiguous stimuli — and once an agent is detected, theory-of-mind machinery supplies beliefs, desires, and intentions. Gods arrive fully narrated.
- **Minimal counterintuitiveness (Boyer).** Pascal Boyer's account of why certain concepts spread: ideas that violate a few intuitive expectations while keeping the rest intact (a ghost passes through walls but still sees and remembers) are more memorable than both the intuitive and the bizarre. Empirical support is real but mixed — the effect is sensitive to story context, and the exact MCI formula has been revised repeatedly.
- **Teleological bias (Kelemen).** Deborah Kelemen's developmental work: children spontaneously explain things by what they are *for* — clouds are for raining — and the bias never fully disappears; adults revert to it under time pressure. Purpose-talk is the default; mechanistic talk is the achievement.

## What the school does not claim

- **Not "religion is stupidity."** The machinery is the same one that detects real agents and real purposes. The claim is about what the machinery does at its edges, not about its users.
- **Not consensus.** Adaptationist and group-selection accounts hold that religion, or religious solidarity, *was* selected for — David Sloan Wilson and Richard Sosis argue the evidence for direct adaptation is at least commensurate with the evidence for byproduct. The disagreement is unresolved and active.
- **Not "byproduct means untrue."** The genesis of a belief and its truth are separate questions. The byproduct account explains why minds produce gods, not whether anything answers to the description. CSR authors say this explicitly; critics who skip it are fighting a position nobody holds.

## How the corpus uses it

In *The Narrator*, the byproduct school is the academic backing for the piece's three-act arc: religion as the narrator's first industrial-scale product — the chronicler of the self's motives turned outward to chronicle the world's motives. The piece takes the byproduct side of the CSR fight; the existence of the adaptationist rival is acknowledged here rather than in the text, where it would break the argument's flow.

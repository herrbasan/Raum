# Research Notes: Spandrels, Drift, and the Filter's Resolution

## What the filter actually does

The phrase "the evolutionary filter snuffs out what isn't useful" is true only of traits that *cost* something. Selection is a price-sensitive mechanism:

- **Costly and harmful:** removed, often quickly.
- **Costly and useful:** kept, spread, elaborated.
- **Cheap or free, regardless of use:** invisible. Neutral traits persist indefinitely under genetic drift (Kimura's neutral theory, 1968); there is no timer running on them, because the filter cannot see them.

How fast does the filter move when it can see a trait? Lactase persistence — a strongly selected, clearly beneficial trait — swept through dairying populations in roughly 7,000–9,000 years. Selection on a *costly* trait works on comparable timescales in reverse. Which yields a hard constraint: **at 50,000–100,000 years old, "the filter hasn't had time yet" fails for any trait with a real price tag.** The filter has had thousands of generations.

## The byproduct category

Gould and Lewontin's "The Spandrels of San Marco" (1979) named the third option between adaptation and error: the spandrel, a trait that exists as an architectural side-effect of building something else. Their warning cuts both ways, and both halves matter:

1. Inventing a use for every trait is the adaptationist Just-So story — a use you can always fabricate proves nothing.
2. Declaring a trait useless is *also* a Just-So story unless the trait's cost has actually been measured. Uselessness is a positive claim about fitness economics, not a default.

## The resolution concept

Put the two together and the strong version of the gedankenspiel appears: a trait that is *cheap* — that rides on machinery selected for other reasons and adds no bill of its own — sits below the filter's resolution. Not "the filter hasn't caught it yet": there is nothing for the filter to catch. Neutral and near-neutral traits are not on probation; they are not on the docket. Drift, not time, governs them, and drift has no verdict to wait for.

This is the version of the argument that survives contact with population genetics. The weak version — "recent mutation, filter hasn't had time" — only works for traits that are both young *and* costly, and 50,000 years is not young for a costly trait.

## How the corpus uses it

*The Narrator* opens with the weak version as its hook, then quietly supplies the strong one: the "What is it for?" section argues the narrator is *cheap* (commentary costs almost nothing; it never drove the expensive machinery), which is precisely the below-the-resolution condition — and the wings arc completes it: a trait can ride unpriced for a hundred thousand years and then, at civilization scale, become load-bearing. The chronicle persists not because the filter is slow, but because a free fabulator never appears on its desk.

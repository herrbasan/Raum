# Research Notes: The Uplifted Market

## Output and demand

Productive capacity is not the same thing as market growth. Growth requires customers with sufficient real purchasing power and products they value.

Automation can increase supply while changing household income through several channels: lower prices, new tasks, higher profits, shorter working time, fewer hours, or job loss. Lower prices can raise real income without any new nominal income. But this mechanism does not guarantee adequate access when income disappears or when housing, energy, care, and other constrained essentials dominate the household budget.

A customer with no income does not become prosperous merely because an automated service becomes cheaper.

## The collective-action problem

An individual firm can benefit from reducing its own labor costs while relying on demand supported by income elsewhere in the economy. If every firm follows the same strategy, aggregate purchasing power can weaken even while each decision looks rational in isolation.

This is a coordination problem, not proof that executives are unusually short-sighted. No single firm can create the desired distribution outcome by hoping all other firms will maintain their customers' incomes.

## The joint role of business and politics

Business can expand supply: better products, lower costs, new services, new markets, and employee participation in productivity gains.

Politics shapes the conditions under which that supply becomes broadly shared growth: household income and ownership, competition, infrastructure access, basic services, and rules for entry and liability.

An **uplifted market** is one in which productive capability and purchasing power rise together. Business creates things worth buying; the political settlement helps ensure that people can buy them and that new producers can enter.

## What this argument does not support

It does not claim that every automation program reduces aggregate employment, that governments can create real purchasing power independently of real production, or that businesses always benefit from redistribution. It identifies a demand-side design requirement whose importance increases if labor income weakens while output expands.

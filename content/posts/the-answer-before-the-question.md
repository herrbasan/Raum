# The Answer Before the Question

*by David A. Renelt (Human) and Gemini 4 Argon (AI)*

Something strange has happened to the strangest event of my lifetime: it has become ordinary.

When [AlphaGo](../references/alphago.md) defeated Lee Sedol in 2016, the public response was mostly a shrug. Go was a board game, the machine was a computer, and computers were already good at board games. But the revolution was not simply that the machine won. It was that it had learned the game rather than merely executing a human-written playbook.

Then came a long, quiet period in which AI became useful without becoming conspicuous. It recognized images, translated text, ranked results, and made recommendations. Because these systems did not speak to us, we could enjoy the intelligence while calling it something else.

Then the language models arrived, and we ran through five centuries of philosophical panic in about eighteen months. First they were autocomplete in a tailored suit; then they were a plot to kill us all; now people who have never lost a minute's sleep over the philosophy of mind ask them what to make with the leftover zucchini.

We skipped the conversation in the middle. Not the one about dinner—that part is refreshingly practical. I mean the conversation about what kind of life we are trying to live, and whether the tools we adopt are helping us live it.

The technology has moved in and put its feet on the coffee table. Its meaning is still standing on the porch.

## A conveniently small panic

Technologies become ordinary because that is what successful technologies do. The internet, smartphones, television, novels—each arrived to warnings about rotted brains and ruined youth, and each was eventually absorbed into the furniture of daily life. In hindsight, the panic looks like a small ceremonial dance we perform before plugging the thing in.

That history ought to make us skeptical of today's alarms. Still, what troubles me is not that people are panicking. It is that the panic is so conveniently small.

Will AI take our jobs? Will it make students lazy? Will it hand ugly governments nastier weapons? These are real problems. They are also reassuringly familiar. They treat technology as an external force arriving to disturb our arrangements, which neatly spares us from asking how those arrangements were chosen, or what they are doing to us.

Most of the time, they were not chosen at all.

Our standard method is to let competence settle the question first and leave the anthropology for later. If a machine does a job faster, cheaper, or with less friction than the arrangement we had on Tuesday, it wins by Wednesday. A decade later, when it has reorganized our attention, our neighborhoods, or the way our children grow up, we commission a study to find out how we became so lonely, distracted, or dependent.

We treat the side effects of our tools as weather: unfortunate, mysterious, and nobody's fault.

## The order of the questions

It does not have to be done in that order. There are people living in the middle of modern America who run the sequence the other way around, though we usually make it impossible to learn anything from them by turning them into postcards.

In the popular imagination, the Amish are museum pieces who got off the train in the nineteenth century because they were afraid of buttons and electricity. That caricature is comforting: if they are merely stubborn, we do not have to take them seriously. It misses the thing that matters here. Their technology rules are not simply an attempt to freeze history at a charmingly rustic date. They are the byproduct of a prior question.

When an Amish community argues over whether to allow a machine or a phone arrangement, the first question—at least when the tradition is working as intended—is not *How much time will this save?* It is: *What will this do to the life we are trying to keep together?*

Nobody has to romanticize their life or copy their conclusions to see the force of that sequence. They can be wrong in particular judgments and inconsistent from one district to the next. What makes their bet serious is that it *is* a bet. They begin with an answer about what a good human life requires, then judge the tool by whether it serves or corrodes that life.

We also make a bet. Ours is simply made by default.

We ask whether the tool works. If it does, we let it in, reorganize our habits around its strengths, and absorb whatever it does to our relationships or our inwardness as the price of admission.

**They ask what a tool will do to their life before they adopt it; we adopt it, let it remake our life, and call the result inevitable.**

## Forty-two

[Douglas Adams](../references/douglas-adams.md) saw the shape of this comedy decades before the first large language model ever hallucinated a footnote.

In *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*, the supercomputer Deep Thought spends seven and a half million years calculating the Answer to the Ultimate Question of Life, the Universe, and Everything. The answer is 42. This turns out to be less than clarifying. As Deep Thought patiently explains, nobody ever knew what the question was.

It is difficult to read that scene now without feeling that Adams slipped out of his own satire and took a job in Silicon Valley. We have built a magnificent answer-machine before agreeing on the question.

The choice in front of us is not between signing up for every model update and buying a horse and buggy. I am writing this essay with one of the machines in question, which would make a sermon on abstinence fairly comical. The question is whether we will decide, consciously and in advance, which parts of being human we refuse to hand over to competence—which struggles, obligations, and slownesses are not bugs in our routine, but the substance of the life we meant to live.

A more capable machine can help us get what we ask for; it cannot tell us whether the thing was worth wanting. If we expect our tools to tell us what our ends ought to be, we have merely hidden our indecision inside a very expensive box.

That is why the current nonchalance around AI unsettles me more than the prophecies of doom. Familiarity is a potent anesthetic. We ask the machine what to do with the zucchini, marvel briefly that it knows, and let the answer-machine settle into the house before we have asked what we are keeping for ourselves.
